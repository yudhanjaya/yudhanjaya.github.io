# In Defense of Pangram
{: .no_toc }

*November 2026*

Recently there have been a number of controversies involving the Ai-writing detector [Pangram](https://www.pangram.com) and prize-winning stories.

- In May 2026, Pangram reported that 100% of Jamir Nazir’s story, *"The Serpent in the Grove"*, which won the Commonwealth Prize, was AI-written.
- In November 2026, Vauhini Vara, writing for the Atlantic, used Pangram to point out that [winner of the Caine Prize](https://www.caineprize.com) - "*the Blue Fighter*" by Simbarashe Steyn Kundizeza - seems to be an AI-written story.

In both cases, the outcry has been bizarre. Despite Jamir Nazir's story having *obvious* AI tells such as a women who "had the kind of walking that made benches become men" or “smiled like sunrise over a sink” -  both prizes doubled down.

Internet commentators cried that this statistical detection of AI writing is somehow racist. There have been claims that AI writes in these stilted patterns precisely because it ingested the writing of black and brown authors from the global south, not that authors have used AI.

Tools like Pangram, rather than being a defense of human writing seemed to have instead been hand-waved away, leaving the field wide open for exploitation.

In the case of the Caine Prize even their statement on Instagram seemed to be AI generated:

![Image](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HW5uG9qX0AA2eXK?format=jpg&name=4096x4096)

> Human centred, evidence led, thorough. Multiple lines of evidence, not a single score. Every account aligned.

***

There are two problems here.

The first is that the people who are rightfully opposed to AI in writing seem to have absolutely no critical engagement with the technologies involved, and therefore no real understanding of current generation capabilities, writing styles or patterns.

We already know that untrained humans are notoriously bad at recognizing AI-generated text. To quote [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing):

> *Do not rely too much on your own judgment. Humans are notoriously bad at distinguishing human and LLM-generated text. While research on humans' abilities to detect AI-generated text is still limited, a 2025 study has shown that human ability to distinguish LLM text from human is no better than random chance. Another 2025 study on German theses has shown that humans managed a "recognition rate of 57 % for AI texts and 64 % for human-generated texts".*
>
> *In a 2025 preprint, a subset of 'expert' annotators correctly identified 90% of AI-generated text (and misidentified 3.3% of human-written text) as AI-generated. Study participants who didn't use LLMs much did only slightly better than random chance (in both directions).*
>
> *Note, also, that human speech and writing is being influenced by LLMs, and thus they are becoming more similar. This was already evident in 2024, as shown by a study that detected a significant LLM influence in spoken content (e.g. conversational podcasts). Further studies seem to confirm this influence on language, including semantics and word choices. However, this is a lagging and patchwork process, and the reverse is not true: LLM output is not becoming more similar to that of human writing.*

Unless you know AI tells, you tend to be pretty bad at recognizing it. 

One would assume that judges of literary awards and literary merit - fundamentally human aspect of writing and the community - would at least be technically competent enough to distinguish machine writing from human, or at least have trained themselves in that regard, but well, one assumes wrong. In fact, the [literary world is sleepwalking into a crisis, as Kelsey Piper so forcefully points out](https://www.theargumentmag.com/p/the-literary-world-is-sleepwalking).

The second, which complicates the matter, is the fact that writing from the global south seems to be expected to be this tortured, heavy, and quite frankly, bizarre strain of navel gazing. Actual readable writing seems to be left to the Anglosphere. 

From the rest of us, it seems the world expects endless meditations using analogies and metaphors that only make sense if you put on some heavy Orientalist glasses and say, oh, *but they're so brown and exotic, their worldviews are so alien*. As if somehow we were a different species of humans altogether, as if we did not use the internet or everyday applications, as if we did not understand Microsoft Word, or have access to thesauruses and dictionaries; as if it is simply not possible to convey our social media without doing the literary equivalent of beating up a thesaurus in an empty parking lot.

***

Let's revisit the case of Jamir Nazir.

![Image](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HIqCs9wbMAAthLQ?format=jpg&name=large)

There is a [very fine article](https://africasacountry.com/2026/05/how-to-read-postcolonial-writing) by Africa as a Country on how to read post-colonial writing, which points out the nature of this otherization and this alien gaze within the literary sphere.

*What exactly were the judges thinking when they selected this story? Surely, there were other, more worthy winners. According to Sharma Taylor, the judge for the Caribbean region, the story was selected for the following reasons:*

> *Jamir Nazir’s language is sublime—precise yet richly evocative—conjuring vivid, lush imagery with remarkable economy. Through sharp sensory detail, he renders the Grove as a living presence, where labour, landscape, and memory are intimately entwined. Polished and confident, this is a story with a melodic voice that lingers long after the final line. Jamir Nazir’s prose pulses with a voice of restraint and quiet authority—a beautifully told and assured piece of storytelling.*

*If these feel like vague and uninspiring platitudes, that’s because they are. It is almost as if someone put postcolonial keywords in a blender and served up the slop. What they reveal is a critical vocabulary that has become entirely decorative—terms like “richly evocative,” “sensory detail,” and “melodic voice” floating free of any engagement with the actual sentences on the page. Nazir’s selection, therefore, is less about him using AI and more about the irony of Euro-American conceptions about the unintelligibility of postcolonial writing. Despite years of literary criticism seeking to undo the idea of the inscrutable Other, here we were. . . .*

***

And the Caine Prize winner?

![Image](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HXWMDC3XQAAHR4Z?format=jpg&name=large)

Much of the anger directed at the Atlantic seems to assume that Vauhini Vara, the author of that article, relied solely on Pangram, but she didn't. That article actually pointed out the AI tells that were obvious and common in AI-produced writing: the repeated spam of *not X but Y* sentence constructions; the glut of metaphors; the aphorisms that didn't make sense, cliched stereotypes and cultural tropes.

[Brunella Urbano](https://thepointmag.com/examined-life/the-part-about-the-machines/) writing for the Point Mag (translated from Spanish), pointed out the exact same patterns in the award-winning novel *Donde el agua trabaja*, by Peruvian writer David Silva Rojas. And again, the reaction to that analysis was identical: allegations of racism, mistrust of statistical methods, "a display of ignorance and obscurantism on the part of many intellectuals from our continent." And Brunella Urbano notes the exact same thing that Vauhini Vara did: this “It’s not X. It’s Y” pattern.

It would be fabulous indeed to believe that all these different writers from different parts of the Global South (remember we are not a monolith), seem to coincidentally write exactly like the state of the art AI models available today. What a fantastic feat of magical realism.

However, one is an interesting anecdote. Two is the start of a suspicious pattern and multiple is a problem.

Using cries of racism to cover up crap like this is in fact racist in itself. 

It is the assumption that writers for whom English is a second language cannot be competent enough to write a sentence that makes sense to another human being. It also infantilizes writers, as if we are some sort of strange beings who are not capable of being liars, cheats, thieves or assholes (when in fact a cursory history of interesting writers will show you the very opposite).

Now, no model is 100% accurate.  I have a statistics and machine learning background: it's a very common aphorism in statistical modeling that if your model is very accurate, then all you've got is an overfit.

But in my opinion, any fool should be able to see the value in using these tools in an age where increasing volumes of text are generated by large language models with - if you know how they work - have ridiculously common tells. So much so that Wikipedia has an [entire section on how to identify signs of AI writing in Wikipedia articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing).

If it's not clear, I'm on the side of Pangram here. People have tested Pangram ([medium post](https://medium.com/@veryfineprint5597/scanning-for-pangram-errors-00033d003246)), [on Twitter](https://x.com/JohnHolbein1/status/2121454993039298813). And not just on Twitter or medium post by random people, there have been [actual studies on the subject](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2026/11/17/the-2026-pangram-social-justice-misinformation-wars-collective-understanding-of-binary-classification-takes-a-hit/).

In fact let me name a few people who are actually studying this. Tuhin Chakrabaty, an assistant professor at Stony Brook University, has studied books that get flagged by Pangram (that is to say 14,419 self published fiction books sold on Amazon from 2023 to 2026 March: see their paper at https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.20349) with Paramveer Dhillon of the University of Michigan, Xinyue Liu of Stony Brooke, and Jane Ginsberg at Columbia Law.

He also ran [(]one hundred and eighty six African novels through Pangram]([)https://tuhinchakrabarty.substack.com/p/are-ai-detectors-really-racist-or).  You won't know what he found? Pangram isn't racist towards African writing. There's zero indicator that Pancram sees African writing and starts screaming.

Pengram is in fact very good at detecting AI slop. If something shows up as 2% AI on Pangram, that can be dismissed as statistical noise if something registers as a hundred percent - well that's time to sit up and pay attention.

Critically, despite Pangram having a open bounty for pre-LLM writing that detects as AI, nobody has so far managed to find such a thing. The only thing so far that the Internet has dredged up is a joke poem where the same 6 words are repeated four thousand times.

Meanwhile, on the other side - aka "pangram is evil" - the best we seem to have were Timnit Gebru's "trust me, bro," claims that she has spoken to students who've said they've been unfairly flagged for AI writing.

The slightly more coherent  counterclaim is a strawman argument: saying statistical tools like this have been pretty bad, and some have even flagged the US Constitution as AI written. Indeed. But are they the same statistical tool? Is a hammer like a spade? 

Look, let me do a Gebru. I know my shit on this. In fact, since 2019, I've been training machine learning models, I've been building my own large language models, and I run AI locally for programming, data extraction and policy research. Hell, I think OpenAI and Anthropic are unambiguously evil, but I'm a massive advocate of open source AI that everyone has access to. 

Hell, I've done this before. I have a post on this site (Can You Vibe-Code A Novel) in which I specifically had Claude generate a 50,000+ word "novel" just to evaluate how far the public interfaces for these tools have come. 

I even have a series of novels, *The Salvage Crew* series, where I deliberately set myself the challenge of using local AI in interestingly oddball ways that I explain in needless detail in the forewords and afterwards. I don't pretend it's high literature, but critically, I'm not trying to fool people.  If I can figure out how to use a tool in an unconventional way, I'm going to talk about it.

TL;DR I cannot tell you how many hundreds of thousands of chats I have seen, the exact same type of language that these so-called literary award-winning writers demonstrate.

So trust me, bro. 

 If this is what wins awards, then we should perhaps shut the awards down and go home - because Claude and ChatGPT and Quen 3.8 flash next and GLM 5.3 and DeepSeek 4.1 and whatever else I've forgotten to mention here -

they've already won.

***

Look, I find this whole thing ironic.

In 2018, I wrote a story for Brian Walton called *The Writing Contest*. In it, a poor writer enters a writing contest. The writer desperately needs money for his mother's care. In a spate of uncertainty, he spins up an AI model and enters like a dozen more entries. And he is utterly crushed when it is the AI-written story that wins.

This is the future that we are in; this is the future that we have been heading into for some time. What I find extremely unpalatable is that the people who desperately want to be guardians of certified organic human written literature are the people least competent at detecting it.

In 2019 I [co-founded a fact checker and grew that into a data journalism lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2022/06/meet-the-fact-checkers-decoding-sri-lankas-meltdown/). One of the things we learned the very hard way was Brandolini's law: *it takes an order of magnitude more effort to refute bullshit than to produce it.*

Whether you like it or not, the problem is at a scale and at a volume that simply cannot be dealt with by people who *aren't* experts at both human and machine constructions.

I honestly think it's about high time that we have more Pangrams. Perhaps each literary prize should in fact concoct, if not a statistical tool, at least a tree of decisions that they can follow to understand whether something is human or not.

Perhaps they should conduct their own independent audits on tools. Perhaps they should have not one, but many, similar to how universities use automated tools to flag plagiarism. Perhaps these tools should be private, so as to prevent future LLMs from learning to game them.

It won't last long. As I always say, if you think you can detect AI writing, that's because you're looking at unsophisticated users. But we do collectively need to start holding ourselves to standards if we are to be serious about this whole business of prizes and literary merit.

Without it, all of this is tosh. Correction: at best it's tosh. At worst it's just Orientalism with extra steps.
